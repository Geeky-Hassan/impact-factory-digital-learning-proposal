## 1. Executive summary

Impact Factory's value sits in the human training experience: attention to the individual, realistic situations, experimentation, confidence and practical changes people can make at work. Its public offer spans open courses, tailored corporate training, intensive programmes and coaching. Its About page emphasises strengths and personal choice. A digital extension should carry that approach into the time before and after live delivery. [S02 — About Us](https://www.impactfactory.com/about-impact-factory/); [S03 — What We Offer](https://www.impactfactory.com/what-we-offer/).

The discovery calls describe an existing support system of resource webpages, email, HubSpot workflows, Zoom/Teams and personal follow-up. The possible gap is continuity: the calls describe preparation, training and follow-up across several tools, without a formal learner journey platform. In corporate programmes, a resource link may pass through an L&D buyer or manager, leaving Impact Factory without a reliable direct touchpoint with the learner. This is a relationship and learning-design problem as much as a technology problem. [D01]

The proposed journey is:

**BEFORE → LIVE → REMEMBER → PRACTISE → APPLY → CONTINUE**

**HUMAN TRAINER REMAINS CENTRAL.** A lightweight learner and company platform would remember the journey underneath these stages. It would help learners arrive prepared, revisit a useful idea, rehearse safely, prepare for a real situation and reconnect with a trainer when appropriate.

Start with one programme, chosen and approved by Impact Factory. A £2,500–£3,500 concept demo over 2–3 weeks could make the idea reviewable. A separately scoped £7,500–£12,000 working pilot over around two months could test it with learners. Agree the scope and useful outcomes at the start, then review before any further investment. Persistent coaching and a broader LMS rollout would follow only if useful evidence supports them. Both ranges and timelines are indicative, subject to scope.

The business opportunity is to extend the usefulness and duration of an existing relationship. A paid extension is one possible route, alongside inclusion in tailored programme packages. The financial illustrations show scale and booking arithmetic; they do not establish demand, profitability or return on investment. MyPath's proposed role is to operate and develop the technology while Impact Factory retains the methodology, client relationship and learning-quality decisions.

## 4. Core gap

**One hypothesis is that a more connected journey could help Impact Factory maintain the learner relationship before and after live training.** This interpretation comes from discovery, remains open to correction and says nothing about the effectiveness of its current training.

Four points to explore:

1. **Before the room:** the trainer may not have a concise, current account of each learner's goal and situation.
2. **After the room:** receiving a resource link does not establish that someone revisited, retrieved or tried anything.
3. **At the moment of need:** preparing for tomorrow's conversation requires a relevant next action, which a library alone may not supply.
4. **Across the relationship:** learner activity, trainer context and company participation are not joined into a coherent record.

More automated emails alone would address distribution. The learning value would come from what happens after the learner opens an activity and how their response informs the next step.

Three implementation routes should remain open during design:

| Route | Useful when | Limitation to test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Improve existing pages and HubSpot workflows | The priority is better invitations, prep and structured follow-up. | May be sufficient for an initial reinforcement journey; persistent practice history needs additional design. |
| Configure an existing learning platform | Standard enrolment, content and reporting needs dominate. | Evaluate fit, administration and total cost before assuming a bespoke build is needed. |
| Add a lightweight managed journey service | Personalised activity, bounded roleplay and context across stages prove valuable. | Requires quality control, data design and a named operator. |

The pilot should discover the minimum useful service. Technology selection follows that evidence.

## 5. Proposed future learner journey

Everything in this section is a **proposal**. Timing is an illustrative starting point, not an evidence-backed optimal schedule.

| Stage | Learner experience | Human trainer's role | What the platform would remember |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **BEFORE — prepare** | Answer a few questions, choose a situation and set a goal. | Review permissioned context and adapt the live session. | Prep response, chosen scenario, goal, sharing preference. |
| **LIVE — learn with people** | Attend Impact Factory training and try approaches with the group. | Facilitate, observe, respond and decide what deserves reinforcement. | Attendance and course/content version; a learner-selected action. |
| **REMEMBER — revisit actively** | Complete a 1–3 minute activity linked to what matters now. | Approve activities, edit wording and handle personal follow-up. | Response, completion and next relevant activity. |
| **PRACTISE — rehearse** | Speak or type through a bounded workplace simulation, then reflect on feedback. | Approve scenarios and criteria; review quality and support the learner. | Practice history and an agreed summary, subject to retention choices. |
| **APPLY — prepare for work** | Ask for help with a real forthcoming situation; review, plan or rehearse. | Remain available for judgement, nuance and deeper development. | Learner-approved context and next action. |
| **CONTINUE — develop further** | Reflect on an attempt, request trainer follow-up or explore another programme. | Interpret development needs and maintain the relationship. | Learner choices, appropriate follow-up and future programme interests. |

**Underneath every stage: a lightweight learner + company platform that remembers the journey.**

For example: prep in the week before training; a chosen action at the live session; brief activities on days 2, 7 and 14; roleplay when useful; and a review around day 30. A tailored programme could later extend this to 60 or 90 days, including support between live sessions. Let learners adjust cadence and return when a workplace need arises.

## 6. BEFORE / REMEMBER / PRACTISE / APPLY

### BEFORE: bring the learner's world into the room

Replace or augment a static preparation document with a short guided interaction:

- “What difficult conversation are you currently avoiding?” Use a fictionalised description without colleague names.
- “What would make this training useful for you?” Save a specific learner goal.
- A brief self-assessment of confidence, clearly presented as self-report.
- Scenario selection, such as missed deadlines, disagreement with a colleague or giving feedback.
- A clear choice about which response the trainer may see.

The trainer receives a concise preparation view: goals, common themes and learner-authorised details. It should help them prepare, with access to original shared responses so an AI summary cannot silently distort the learner's intention. A learner can skip a sensitive question, revise an answer or choose a supplied fictional scenario.

The invitation should explain the purpose: “Help your trainer shape the session around the situations that matter to you.” Test whether the answers are useful enough to justify the learner and trainer time.

### REMEMBER: a small interaction, reached through a notification

Email, Teams or another agreed channel is the **notification**. The learning happens in an activity opened from it. An email open is not learning completion.

Possible 1–3 minute activities include a trainer-approved 30–60 second recap video followed by one question; a multiple-choice scenario; a reflection; a tiny workplace challenge; or a short retrieval quiz. A learner's answer can lead to a different explanation, an easier example, a later prompt or an invitation to practise. A video is optional; text and a good question can be the first version.

**Illustrative content, requiring Impact Factory approval:**

“Alex has missed two deadlines. Which opening would you use in this difficult conversation?”

| Choice | Example opening | Proposed immediate feedback |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | “You clearly don't care about the deadlines.” | Explore what this assumes about Alex's intentions. Try describing the events before interpreting motives. |
| B | “The last two deadlines were missed. Can we talk about what happened and agree a way forward?” | This gives a specific starting point and invites Alex's perspective. How would you express it naturally in your own words? |
| C | “It's probably nothing, so let's leave it.” | Consider whether the issue remains unresolved. What would help you raise it with clarity and care? |

The production feedback must be grounded in approved Impact Factory material, linked to the relevant course idea and open to context. These are draft examples, not an asserted Impact Factory framework or a universal right answer.

After the response: “Write the first sentence you would actually use.” Save only the information needed for the agreed journey. If the learner struggles, offer a recap and another attempt. If they feel ready, offer the roleplay or a small workplace action. Trainers can pause scheduled prompts or replace them with a personal message.

Smart nudges could also offer trainer-approved video recaps, personalised mini-lessons or a reminder to revisit practice. Personalisation would use course, goals and permitted activity responses; it does not justify uncontrolled generated teaching content. Reminders are invitations, not proof of learning. [P02]

### PRACTISE: roleplay first, feedback afterwards

Example: following Difficult Conversations, the learner practises with **Alex, a fictional employee who has missed deadlines**. The learner speaks or types naturally; a text version should be available even if voice is later introduced.

The proposed experience has distinct states:

1. **Brief:** explain the situation, learner objective, difficulty and what will be retained. Let the learner stop or choose another level.
2. **Roleplay:** AI plays Alex and responds within an approved character and scenario. **It must not coach, score or suggest the learner's next line while playing the employee.**
3. **End simulation:** the learner or agreed session limit ends the scene. A visible transition makes the change of role clear.
4. **Separate evaluation:** a different evaluation instruction/process reviews the exchange against trainer-approved criteria and returns evidence-linked feedback. This separation does not by itself guarantee accuracy; trainer calibration remains necessary.
5. **Reflection and retry:** the learner chooses a useful adjustment and can repeat the scene or take a smaller practice step.

If a learner asks for coaching mid-scene, Alex should not suddenly become the coach. Offer a pause/end control outside the character conversation; feedback follows that transition. Inappropriate or distressing content can stop the simulation rather than maintaining character at any cost.

| Level | Alex's behaviour | Learning-design boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1: receptive | Willing to talk and consider the issue. | Establish a usable approach and confidence. |
| 2: defensive | Questions the framing or explains competing priorities. | Respond to resistance while maintaining the relationship. |
| 3: challenging/emotional | Expresses frustration or stronger emotion within an approved script boundary. | Learner chooses readiness; easy stop/debrief; avoid gratuitous escalation. |

Candidate feedback criteria for trainers to adapt: clarity about the situation; curiosity and listening; acknowledgement of the other perspective; managing blame; and agreeing a useful next step. These are proposed criteria, not a claim that Impact Factory already uses this rubric.

Feedback should identify a strength, show the relevant words from the exchange, offer a possible alternative and suggest one next practice goal. It should allow multiple effective styles. Avoid personality labels, definitive claims about emotional state, employment judgements and public leaderboards. “Not enough evidence in this exchange” is a valid result.

Difficulty progression is part of the wider hypothesis. The first working pilot should use one approved scenario at Level 1 unless the final scope explicitly includes more. The proposal can illustrate progression without implying all levels are included in the pilot.

### APPLY: an Impact Factory Digital Coach at the moment of need

A proposed persistent coach could respond to: **“I have to give someone difficult feedback tomorrow.”**

With the learner's knowledge and agreed permissions, it could use their course, training date, goals, previous practice and reviewed feedback to offer three choices:

- **Review the framework:** reopen the relevant approved course material and a short reminder.
- **Plan the conversation:** clarify the desired outcome, opening, likely responses and next step.
- **Practise the conversation:** enter a clearly bounded roleplay, then receive separate feedback.

It should confirm whether remembered context is still relevant and let the learner correct or remove it. “Strengths” and “areas needing reinforcement” should be contextual practice observations and learner goals, not fixed traits or an employer performance profile.

The coach would extend access to Impact Factory's approach. Human trainers remain responsible for the methodology and available for deeper support. Show an explicit route to request that support, with an agreed response process. Complex employment disputes or situations outside the approved learning scope should lead to the appropriate human contact rather than confident advice from the system.

For this proposal, demonstrate these choices with fictional, prewritten content. Persistent memory and open-ended coaching require a later quality and privacy decision.

### AI persona, avatar and voice options

Both proposed AI roleplay and the Digital Coach could be live, conversational personas: text, spoken interaction or an on-screen avatar. An approved licensed voice/character is an option; an instructor’s likeness and voice could be used only with explicit consent, agreed rights, approval and governance. These are optional presentation choices, not a promise to clone a trainer in the pilot. The roleplay actor still stays in character until the session ends; coaching and evaluation remain separate. Test latency, conversation quality, accessibility and costs before agreeing a voice/avatar pilot scope. The persistent coach remains outside the first pilot unless explicitly rescoped. The webpage contains scripted examples and format previews, with no generated speech, avatar video or live AI. [P02]

## 7. Learner-platform concept: a lightweight LMS

**Working direction:** an Impact Factory-branded learning hub on a suitable LMS foundation, with custom reinforcement and AI experiences connected where useful. One account could carry a learner across enrolled company and public courses; this does not grant access to every course automatically. Keep the direct next activity prominent.

Flow: enrol in a course/cohort → prepare and attend live training → receive approved reinforcement and practice → review and continue with a trainer or another programme.

| Audience | Useful proposed LMS functions | Potential benefit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Learner | Enrolled-course home, session details, approved videos/resources, next lesson, practice and personal history | A familiar place to return across programmes. |
| Company / L&D | Invitations, cohort allocation, enrolment/attendance and permissioned activity reporting by course | A view across its learning investment without private conversations. |
| Impact Factory team | Shared preparation, approved content versions, course templates, follow-up scheduling and an approval queue | Reuse the journey structure while retaining programme and trainer judgement. |

Existing LMS foundations warrant evaluation before rebuilding enrolment, access and completion tracking. Moodle's official [LMS features](https://docs.moodle.org/en/Features) and [Workplace multi-tenancy documentation](https://docs.moodle.org/en/Multi-tenancy) illustrate relevant patterns. Workplace is a distinct, partner-delivered product; do not assume its company-separation features are part of ordinary Moodle LMS. This is a design inference, not a vendor selection. Check company isolation, integration effort, usability, support ownership, licensing and total cost during pilot design. No new vendor purchase or deployment is proposed here. [S17–S18]

The company example can filter two fictional programmes. Difficult Conversations has 20 enrolments / 18 attendances / 15 activations / 12 reinforcement completions / 9 practice completions. Presentation Skills has 12 / 11 / 10 / 8 / 6. Combined totals are 32 / 29 / 25 / 20 / 15, counting course enrolments, not unique people. These values are **PROPOSED CONCEPT** data, not results. One person on both courses counts twice. The pilot still starts with one programme.


The platform matters because each activity can otherwise become another disconnected link. A small persistent record allows the learner to resume, lets relevant content follow their choices, and gives authorised staff enough context to support the journey.

| Information | Purpose | Proposed access/default |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Learner identity, company and cohort membership | Correct enrolment and company separation. | Minimum necessary data; verify company membership before granting access. |
| Course, training date and attendance | Connect the extension to the actual programme. | Learner and authorised programme administrators. |
| Prep responses and goals | Make training relevant. | Learner; selected responses shared with the trainer by an explicit workflow. |
| Activities, nudges and next activity | Resume and choose an appropriate next step. | Learner; service-level activity status for authorised operators. |
| Practice sessions and feedback | Support repeat practice and reflection. | Private by default; an explicit, limited sharing route to a trainer. |
| Strengths and reinforcement needs | Remember useful observations. | Learner-editable summaries tied to evidence and date. |
| Preferences, permissions and retention choices | Honour notification, sharing and memory decisions. | Learner controls with auditable administration. |

The learner home would answer four questions: “What did I learn?”, “What matters to me?”, “What could I try next?” and “How do I get human help?” It should not require browsing a large catalogue to reach a two-minute activity.

For corporate delivery, the buyer agrees the invitation route. Each delegate claims their own access through an appropriate individual invitation; a manager-forwarded resource link alone should not grant access to private records. Give staff visibility of invitation failures and a personal recovery process. Separate access to training from optional future marketing.

For the pilot, use a controlled roster and a minimal journey record. Keep existing booking and HubSpot processes where workable. A later integration could exchange enrolment and completion status; raw conversations should not flow into CRM records by default. Teams notifications, booking sync, SSO and other integrations remain scope decisions, not promised capabilities.

Before production, agree identity handling, organisation separation, access controls, deletion/export, retention periods, incident handling, model-provider terms and permitted data use. Do not use learner conversations to train general models by default. Record these decisions in the operating agreement and learner-facing explanation.

## 8. Corporate dashboard concept

The corporate view should help an L&D buyer coordinate a programme and understand participation. It should not turn private rehearsal into employee surveillance.

| Dashboard area | Appropriate proposed information | Permission boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Programme overview | Bookings, delivery dates, programme and cohort. | Only the client's authorised accounts. |
| Delegate administration | Invited/enrolled delegates and attendance. | Named records only where operationally necessary and agreed. |
| Journey completion | Required activities completed, due and outstanding. | Define completion in advance; named status only when justified and disclosed. |
| Engagement | Aggregate participation, return visits and activity uptake over a defined period. | Display denominator and date range; do not label this a skill score. |
| Activities completed | Counts by activity type and programme. | Avoid revealing sensitive scenario choices. |
| Programme reporting | Cohort-level participation and opt-in evaluation summaries. | No raw chat, voice recordings, individual AI assessments or personal goals by default. |

Small groups can make people identifiable even in aggregates. Agree minimum reporting group sizes, suppress or combine small cells, and prevent filters or exports from reconstructing individuals. Until that policy is settled, exclude sensitive practice analytics from the corporate report.

A learner could choose to share a specific action plan or trainer-reviewed summary through a clearly explained process. Employer access requires an explicit purpose, appropriate permissions and privacy review; a blanket tick box is not a substitute for that design. Routine access should follow role and necessity, including for MyPath support staff.

A report might say how many invited learners attended, completed preparation or tried an activity. It cannot establish workplace behaviour change solely from clicks, completion or AI-generated scores.

`Adopted learners = 5,000 × adoption rate`

`Illustrative gross sales = adopted learners × assumed price per learner`

### 10.4 Costs, unit economics and other assumptions

The demo and pilot estimates in section 14 are potential **costs to Impact Factory**; any future service price would be agreed after reviewing results. The learner prices above are hypothetical **sales prices to its customers**. They are different commercial relationships and should never be combined into a claim about margin without a cost model.

Use this structure after obtaining pilot data:

`Incremental contribution = collected digital sales − agreed technology/service costs − AI/voice usage − hosting/support not included in the fee − content/trainer review time − onboarding/integration costs − attributable selling/admin costs`

`Indicative break-even learners = fixed incremental costs ÷ (net realised price per learner − variable cost per learner)`

The second calculation is meaningful only when the denominator is positive and the cost boundary and period are defined. No break-even result is asserted here because the necessary costs, usage and realised price are unknown.

## 11. Learning-design evidence

Use a small body of research to justify the design choices, with an explicit boundary between published evidence and this product hypothesis.

| Research | What the evidence supports | Design implication and limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Taylor, Russ-Eft & Chan (2005), *A meta-analytic review of behavior modeling training*. Review of **117 studies**. | Transfer was stronger where practice included trainee-generated scenarios, alongside other design and workplace conditions. Learning effects were larger than job-behaviour and organisational-results effects. | Invite real learner situations, rehearse and set a workplace action. The moderator findings do not isolate an AI effect, and practice alone does not guarantee transfer. [S14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16060787/). |
| Pan & Rickard (2018), *Transfer of test-enhanced learning: Meta-analytic review and synthesis*. **122 experiments; 10,382 participants**. | Retrieval practice can support transfer beyond the original test; benefits vary with task and retrieval conditions. | Ask learners to recall or apply an idea, then respond to their answer. Do not equate quiz performance with skilled workplace conversation. [S15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29733621/). |
| Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie (2020), *The Power of Feedback Revisited*. | Educational feedback research shows substantial variation; information content matters, and effects differ across outcome types. | Give specific, useful feedback with a next attempt. Evidence about educational feedback does not validate AI feedback accuracy or adult workplace behaviour change. [S16](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087/full). |

Our learning-design inference is to connect personally relevant preparation, active recall, rehearsal, useful feedback and opportunities to try something at work. The proposed short durations and schedule are usability hypotheses. These studies do not prove that Impact Factory's future digital or AI experience will reproduce their results, and no study effect size is used in the sales model.

Training transfer also depends on a chance to apply the skill and the workplace context. Ask learners what they tried and what helped or blocked them. Where appropriate, invite a supportive manager conversation without disclosing private practice or making the learner's AI record part of performance management.

### Concept demo / MVP — review the idea

Indicative: **£2,500–£3,500 over 2–3 weeks**, after scope/content approval. Here MVP means a bounded concept demonstration, not a production learner platform.

Scope: map one programme’s preparation, live session and follow-up; create clickable learner and company screens; show one recap or mini-lesson with a smart-nudge example; illustrate one scripted roleplay, separate feedback and possible voice/avatar formats. Deliver a reviewable demo and an outline for a working pilot, shaped by trainer feedback.

Production AI, integrations and real learner data are outside the demo scope. Review the experience with Impact Factory before deciding whether it warrants a learner pilot.

### Working pilot — test with learners

Indicative: **£7,500–£12,000 over around two months**, after scope/content approval. Agree build, learner-use and follow-up windows in the schedule.

Proposed scope: one programme; approximately 30–50 learners across suitable small cohorts; simple learner access; preparation; approved content and smart follow-up; one live AI roleplay scenario with separate feedback; basic learner records and company reporting; trainer approval and a named support owner. Agree text, voice or avatar scope and any likeness permissions before quoting; every format is not assumed to be included. A full course rollout and persistent open-ended Digital Coach are outside this scope.

Before learner use, approve the screens, content, invitation route and feedback criteria. Agree data handling, total budget, usage allowance, support and escalation. ### Evidence to review

| Question | Useful evidence | Limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Did learners reach the journey? | Delivered invitations / eligible invited; activated / successfully invited. | Separate access problems from lack of interest. |
| Was preparation useful? | Completions / invited; trainer feedback on relevance. | Completion alone is not better learning. |

Review learner use, trainer confidence in content/feedback, corporate client value and operating effort against the agreed outcomes. Refine, extend or stop. If the pilot gives Impact Factory what it needs, define the next scope, service responsibilities and price together. No long-term price or rollout commitment is requested now.

Agree a single service contact, response hours, escalation route, change allowance, approval turnaround and usage cap.
